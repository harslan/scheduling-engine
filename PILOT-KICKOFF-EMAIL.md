**To:** Dean Tracey Riley
**Subject:** A fairer way to handle SBS office space — ready to show you

Dear Tracey,

This began with the office-sharing question you raised this spring, and since July a small group of us has turned it into working software. I want to show you what we built, and I'll keep it plain.

The problem is faculty office space. As SBS grows, there aren't enough private offices to give everyone their own, and the usual ways of deciding who sits where — seniority, negotiation, whoever asks loudest — leave people feeling the process wasn't fair. That feeling, more than the shortage itself, is the hard part.

So we built software that takes the fairness question off the table. It works from one simple idea: **an office is earned by your teaching schedule, and the same rule is applied to everyone by the same arithmetic.** A faculty member says which days they're on campus; the system assigns offices so that people who are never in on the same days can share one without ever colliding; and every person can see exactly why they got the office they got. Two colleagues can trade, but only if everyone affected agrees — no one's arrangement changes without their say-so.

Three things make it trustworthy, and they're the whole point:

- **It's transparent.** Everyone sees the rule and their own result. Nothing is decided behind a curtain.
- **Nothing happens without consent.** No one is moved without agreeing to it.
- **You're in control, and nothing is binding yet.** The system won't make a single assignment "official" until the faculty have actually voted to adopt the rule.

Here's the part that convinced me it was real: we ran it on this past spring's actual teaching schedule — all 120 faculty, under made-up names to protect privacy — and it produced the same answer our earlier analysis did, down to the number: 61 offices needed, 60 available, short by exactly one, and it told us who. The math and the software agree.

And I want to go first. I don't need a dedicated office, and I'd rather prove the idea with my own than ask it of anyone else first. I'd like to give mine up and put it into the system as a shared room that any faculty member can book — myself included, whenever I need space, on the same terms as everyone else. If we're going to ask colleagues to share, the first office in the pool should be mine. It makes the point better than anything I could write here.

None of this commits the School to anything else. I'd just like to show it to you — about fifteen minutes — so you can see it work and decide whether it's worth taking a step further. If it is, the first real step is small: a short conversation with you and a couple of pieces of information from Brenda and the School, and even then everything runs quietly alongside the real world, with nothing set in stone.

Could we find fifteen or twenty minutes in the next week or two? I'll come to you.
